A good flight plan is half of a calm flight. Let us go through it in order: route, altitudes, fuel and weather.

## How to choose the route

The shortest path between two airports is a great circle. In a planner you only need to enter the airports, and the track is built for you with intermediate navigation fixes added — points such as **IG** or **BOB** that the airway runs through. Keep in mind:

- airspace restrictions and areas where a controller expects a specific track;
- intermediate alternate airports along the way in case something goes wrong;
- actual distance: the plan must be flyable, not just pretty.

## SIDs, STARs and approach fixes

A **SID** links the runway to the airway, a **STAR** links the airway to the approach area. The rule is simple: choose the ones that lead towards your route rather than the shortest ones. Open the charts before the flight and mark:

- the first departure fix with its altitude or speed restrictions;
- the STAR entry fixes, where the descent and any holding begin;
- the frequencies at which the handoffs between controllers happen.

## Flight levels and RVSM

The flight level depends on the magnetic course: opposite flows use **even and odd series** of levels so that aircraft never cruise at the same altitude. Below FL290 levels are stepped by 2000 feet; in RVSM airspace (roughly FL290 to FL410) the step is 1000 feet, with full compliance with the RVSM rules. Practical advice: weigh the cost of a climb — on a short leg the opposite level only pays off in turbulence or for real fuel savings.

## Calculating fuel

Fuel is built up in layers, never estimated by eye:

1. **Trip** — for the flight itself, at the planned altitude and weight.
2. **Taxi** — for taxi and engine run-up.
3. **Contingency** — the unexpected: wind, holding, deviation.
4. **Alternate** — the flight to the alternate airport.
5. **Reserve** — the mandatory reserve, which must never be touched.

## Weights and limitations

Check takeoff weight, landing weight and the limit speeds for your type. Excess mass reduces climb performance and lengthens the landing roll — on a virtual aircraft it honestly breaks the flight.

## Weather, NOTAMs and the alternate

Twenty-four hours and again one hour before departure, check **METAR** and **TAF** along the route, then the **NOTAMs**: runway closures, navaid work and area restrictions all change the plan. If the forecast at the destination is unreliable, pick the alternate early and put its fuel in the block.
