Radio work online is not an exam in public speaking — it is a working tool. Clarity matters to the controller, not elegance: a short call in the right structure beats a long story every time.

## What a message is made of

Every transmission follows the **who to — who is speaking — what** pattern:

1. **Who to** — the addressee: the controller's callsign (ground, tower, center) or your own callsign when you are answering.
2. **Who is speaking** — your callsign, stated at the start.
3. **What** — the content: a request, a confirmation, a report.

Example: "Ground, SkyNational 1234, request taxi to runway 27". Who to — ground, who is speaking — SkyNational 1234, what — a taxi request. The callsign goes at the beginning and at the end of a long instruction so that it cannot be missed.

## Key pilot phrases

- **Calling a controller:** "Ground, SkyNational 1234, stand 12, request taxi".
- **Taxi:** "Taxiing to runway 27", or the readback: "Runway 27 via Charlie, SkyNational 1234".
- **Takeoff clearance:** "Cleared for takeoff runway 27" — confirmation, nothing more.
- **Level change:** "Climbing to flight level 120" / "Descending to 4000 feet".
- **Handoff:** "Contact approach on 124.35" → "124.35, goodbye".
- **Descent and landing:** "Descend and maintain 3000 feet", "Cleared ILS runway 27".

Read an instruction back in the same order the controller gave it — they are checking your reply against their own readout.

## Speaking speed

Speak more slowly than feels natural. Read digits in pairs: "one two — four three" for frequency 124.35. The pause between the instruction and your readback gives the controller time to write it down. If you feel nervous, say the phrase to yourself first and only then transmit.

## If you did not understand

Never guess. The standard answer is **"Say again"** (or "say again, please"). Useful options:

- "Say again last" — repeat the last instruction.
- "Say again the runway" — clarify only the runway number.
- "Confirm runway 27" — check your own understanding.

The controller does not consider this an error; executing an instruction you never understood is.

## Typical newcomer mistakes

- Transmitting without a callsign, or with it only at the end of the sentence.
- Long "just in case" messages instead of a short answer on point.
- Answering the wrong controller: check who the instruction was addressed to.
- Reading frequencies and altitudes faster than they can be written down on the other end.
- Silence instead of "say again" — the most dangerous option, because it turns into a missed instruction.
